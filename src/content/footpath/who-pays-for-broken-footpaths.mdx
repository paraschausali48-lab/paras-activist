India's Ministry of Road Transport and Highways publishes annual road accident data. The 2022 report shows pedestrians accounted for approximately **17% of all road accident deaths** - the largest single victim category, ahead of two-wheeler riders.

This is not evenly distributed across the population.

## The class dimension of road danger

Urban road accident data, when disaggregated, tells a clear story:

- **Who walks?** Domestic workers, daily-wage labourers, street vendors, schoolchildren, and elderly individuals without access to private transport. Not the urban professional class.
- **Where are footpaths broken?** In low-income residential areas and commercial zones where pedestrian traffic is highest, but municipal investment is lowest.
- **Who dies?** The same groups who walk.

The Praja Foundation's 2022 Mumbai audit found that over **60% of footpaths** surveyed were encroached, broken, or absent. The audit areas included low and middle-income wards.

## The economic counter-argument

When activists demand better footpaths, the common counter-argument is affordability. The World Bank's 2019 analysis of road accident costs provides the direct rebuttal:

Road accidents cost India between **3-5% of GDP annually**. That is roughly Rs 6-10 lakh crore at current GDP levels - greater than the entire annual defence budget.

The majority of this cost comes from:
1. Working-age deaths and disability (lost productivity)
2. Emergency healthcare costs borne by public health systems
3. Lost wages for victim families

Investing in pedestrian infrastructure is not a cost. It is a return on investment that more than pays for itself in reduced accident costs, healthcare spending, and productivity losses.

## What needs to change

The evidence points to three concrete, implementable demands:

1. **Encroachment removal with enforcement.** Footpaths cannot be used as informal parking, hawking spaces, or construction storage without alternative provision for those displaced.

2. **Kerb cuts as a legal requirement.** All new and repaired footpaths must include kerb cuts for wheelchair users, disabled individuals, and parents with prams - as mandated under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 but inconsistently implemented.

3. **Accountability at the ward level.** Footpath maintenance is a civic body responsibility. Ward councillors must be made publicly accountable for the condition of footpaths in their wards, with annual audits published online.
